Healthy Habits

Avoid harmful behaviors and build positive routines that support your long-term well-being.

Understanding Habits

Habits—both good and bad—shape our lives more than we realize. They run on autopilot, requiring little conscious thought, which makes them both powerful and challenging to change.

The habit loop consists of:

  • Cue: The trigger that initiates the behavior (a time, place, emotion, or preceding action).
  • Routine: The behavior itself—what you actually do.
  • Reward: The benefit you get from the behavior, which reinforces the habit.

Understanding this loop is key to both breaking bad habits and building good ones. By identifying cues and finding healthier routines that provide similar rewards, you can reshape your automatic behaviors.

Avoiding Harmful Substances

Substance use in young adulthood can have lasting consequences. Understanding risks helps you make informed choices.

Tobacco and Nicotine

Despite declining overall smoking rates, nicotine use remains common among young adults, especially through vaping:

90% of smokers start before age 18
480K deaths per year from smoking (US)
50% of long-term smokers die from smoking-related causes

Key facts:

  • Nicotine is highly addictive—as addictive as heroin or cocaine.
  • Vaping is not safe. E-cigarettes contain harmful chemicals and still deliver addictive nicotine.
  • Quitting gets harder the longer you use. If you haven't started, don't. If you have, quitting now dramatically reduces health risks.
  • Resources for quitting: talk to your doctor, use nicotine replacement therapy, try apps designed to help quit smoking.

Alcohol

Alcohol is deeply embedded in social culture, but its risks are often underestimated:

  • Short-term risks: Impaired judgment, accidents, alcohol poisoning, risky behaviors.
  • Long-term risks: Liver disease, heart problems, increased cancer risk, mental health issues, addiction.
  • Brain development: The brain continues developing until around age 25. Heavy drinking during this time can cause lasting damage.

If you choose to drink, do so responsibly:

  • Know the recommended limits (no more than 4 drinks per occasion, 14 per week for men; 3 per occasion, 7 per week for women).
  • Never drink and drive.
  • Pace yourself and alternate with water.
  • Don't use alcohol to cope with stress or emotions.
  • Be aware of your family history—genetics play a role in alcohol use disorder.

Other Substances

Be informed about risks associated with other substances:

  • Cannabis: While increasingly legal, it can affect memory, motivation, and mental health, especially with heavy use during brain development years.
  • Prescription drug misuse: Taking medications not prescribed to you or differently than prescribed is dangerous and can lead to addiction.
  • Stimulants: Using ADHD medications for studying or performance is risky and ineffective long-term.
  • Recreational drugs: Street drugs often contain dangerous adulterants. Fentanyl contamination has made many substances potentially fatal.

Signs of Problematic Use

Needing more to get the same effect, using to cope with emotions, continued use despite negative consequences, inability to cut down, and withdrawal symptoms when not using are all warning signs.

Building Positive Habits

Creating good habits is about making positive behaviors automatic. Here's how to make habits stick:

  • Start tiny: Begin with habits so small they're impossible to fail. Want to exercise? Start with one push-up.
  • Stack habits: Attach new habits to existing ones. "After I pour my morning coffee, I will write in my journal."
  • Design your environment: Make good habits easy and bad habits hard. Put your running shoes by the door. Keep junk food out of the house.
  • Track your progress: Visual tracking (like a habit tracker) provides motivation and accountability.
  • Never miss twice: Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new (bad) habit.
  • Reward yourself: Celebrate small wins to reinforce the behavior.

Breaking Bad Habits

Changing ingrained behaviors is challenging but possible:

  • Identify triggers: What cues prompt the habit? Stress? Boredom? Certain people or places?
  • Find substitutes: Replace the bad habit with a healthier behavior that provides a similar reward.
  • Change your environment: Remove cues and make the habit harder to perform.
  • Tell others: Social accountability helps. Tell friends and family about your goals.
  • Plan for setbacks: Relapses are part of change. Have a plan for getting back on track.
  • Address underlying issues: Sometimes bad habits mask deeper problems. Consider whether therapy might help.
  • Be patient: Habits take weeks to months to change. Persistence matters more than perfection.

The 21-Day Myth

You may have heard it takes 21 days to form a habit. Research actually suggests it takes 18-254 days, with an average of 66 days. Don't give up too soon.

Daily Routines That Support Health

Building structure into your day creates a foundation for well-being:

Morning routine ideas:

  • Wake at a consistent time
  • Avoid phone for the first 30 minutes
  • Hydrate with water before coffee
  • Move your body—even a few minutes of stretching
  • Eat a nutritious breakfast
  • Review your priorities for the day

Evening routine ideas:

  • Set a consistent bedtime
  • Dim lights 1-2 hours before sleep
  • Limit screens in the evening
  • Prepare for the next day (clothes, lunch, etc.)
  • Reflect on the day—journaling or gratitude practice
  • Wind down with relaxing activities

When to Seek Help

If you're struggling with substance use or behavioral habits you can't control on your own, seek professional help:

  • Talk to your doctor or a mental health professional
  • Consider addiction counseling or rehabilitation programs
  • Join support groups (AA, NA, SMART Recovery, etc.)
  • Call helplines for immediate support

Seeking help is a sign of strength. Addiction and deeply ingrained habits often require professional support to overcome. There's no shame in asking for help.

Resources

SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7 support for substance use and mental health)
